Chapter 1 of 10

Everyday cruise control can waste battery

Adaptive cruise control (ACC) keeps your car a set distance behind the car in front. It speeds up and slows down on its own.

A published study of real ACC cars, from the people who made the OpenACC data set, reports that commercial ACC cars used 2.7% to 20.5% more tractive energy than human drivers. Tractive energy is the energy that turns the wheels.

We ask one question: can an ACC that knows about the battery do better?

Chapter 2 of 10

We use real traffic and real battery measurements

The traffic data is OpenACC, recorded by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre. One real car follows another and the speeds are logged 10 times a second. We cut it into 120-second windows: 203 windows from 5 campaigns, recorded on 9 days.

Each day sits in only one group. 43 windows (fold 0) are used for tuning. 160 windows are kept for the final test. In the test, the car behind was a commercial ACC in 79 windows, a human driver in 40, and a mix in 41.

For the battery we use BMW i3 measurements from TU Munich: 68 trips, 28.0 hours. We split them by trip into 5 folds, so the battery model is always tested on trips it never saw.

Chapter 3 of 10

Physics plus machine learning predicts battery power a little better than physics alone

Every energy number here comes from one BMW i3 battery model. It starts from physics: the force needed to move the car gives the power at the wheels, and a fitted formula turns that into battery power.

Physics is not exact, so a machine learning (ML) model learns the leftover error. This is called a residual model. We score it with RMSE (root mean square error), the typical error in kW. Lower is better.

On trips it had not seen, physics alone is off by 4.37 kW. Physics plus LightGBM (a method that builds many small decision trees) is off by 4.03 kW. That is 7.7% better. We hoped for 30%, so the gain is modest. The ML models are closer to each other than the spread between folds, so none is clearly the best.

We use LightGBM because it had the lowest error and it is fast: 5.0 microseconds per prediction (KNN 197.3, SVR 231.3). With no physics at all it still reaches 4.14 kW. The controllers use the physics part; the ML model is tested on its own.

Chapter 4 of 10

Acceleration and speed matter most, and more data helps

To find out which inputs matter, we shuffled one group of inputs at a time and measured how much the error grew. This is called permutation importance. A bigger number means the model relies on that input more.

Acceleration matters most and speed comes second. They held these places in all 5 folds. Cabin heating, road grade and the temperatures come next, but their order changes from fold to fold, so only “acceleration, then speed, then the rest” is firm.

More data also helps. With 11,509 samples the error is 4.60 kW. With 161,482 it is 4.03 kW. It takes about 52,486 samples before physics plus LightGBM (4.28 kW) beats physics alone (4.37 kW). With 26,809 samples or fewer it is worse than physics alone.

Chapter 5 of 10

Six controllers drive behind the same lead cars

Each controller drives a simulated BMW i3 behind the recorded lead car. Four are baselines, one is our design car and one is a ceiling.

Why MPC? It plans ahead with the battery model, and its limits are part of the plan: the safe gap, the braking and acceleration limits and the regen cap. Regen (regenerative braking) sends braking energy back to the battery, up to a limit. The project chose MPC for these hard limits (decision D2) and added RL as a comparison.

Tuned ACC

Baseline
−3.01%vs the recorded cars, 160 windows (median)

How it thinks. Keeps a set gap behind the lead car (5 m plus 1.5 seconds of driving) and corrects the gap error and the speed difference. It does not look ahead.

Good. Simple, with 0 unsafe windows.

Not so good. It follows far back: mean gap 39.6 m (50.0 m in the 79 commercial-ACC windows), against 40.0 m for the recorded cars.

SMC

Baseline
−2.79%vs the recorded cars, 160 windows (median)

How it thinks. Sliding-mode control pushes a mix of the gap error and the speed error toward zero, harder the further off it is. No look-ahead.

Good. Simple, with 0 unsafe windows.

Not so good. No better than the tuned ACC, and a little less smooth: jerk 0.24 against 0.21.

Plain SAC

Reinforcement learning
−2.28%vs the recorded cars, 160 windows (median)

How it thinks. Soft actor-critic (SAC) is reinforcement learning (RL): a neural network learns by trial and error, over many simulated runs, which acceleration to choose.

Good. Learns its own driving style (seed 42 shown).

Not so good. Unsafe windows: 2, 6 and 37 for the 3 training seeds. Jerk 0.42, the roughest. Not better than the tuned ACC on energy.

Safe SAC

Reinforcement learning + safety filter
−2.50%vs the recorded cars, 160 windows (median)

How it thinks. The same SAC, but a filter (a control barrier function) checks every action against the safe gap, 4 m plus 0.7 s of speed, and overrides it if needed. The filter is also used in training.

Good. 0 unsafe windows in all 3 seeds (seed 42 shown).

Not so good. Still no better than the tuned ACC on energy, and it follows at 43.2 m. The filter steps in on 24.15% of test steps.

MPC, constant-speed preview

Our design car
−2.47%vs the recorded cars, 160 windows (median)

How it thinks. Model predictive control (MPC) plans the next 15 seconds with the battery model and picks the speeds that use the least energy. It assumes the lead car keeps its current speed, and it re-plans every 0.5 seconds. The safe gap, the braking and acceleration limits and the regen cap (35 kW) are part of the plan.

Good. 0 unsafe windows. In the 79 commercial-ACC windows its gap is 41.3 m, close to the recorded cars (40.0 m).

Not so good. Against the tuned ACC it uses 0.47% more energy (160 windows). Learned guesses of the lead car's speed did not help it.

MPC oracle

Ceiling, not a real car
−5.99%vs the recorded cars, 160 windows (median)

How it thinks. The same MPC, but it is told the lead car's true future speed. No real car knows that.

Good. Shows how much planning could save at best.

Not so good. Cannot be built. Learned forecasts did not recover any of this saving on real windows.

Chapter 6 of 10

We wrote the rules before we ran the tests

A result is easy to bend after you have seen it. So each test has a pre-registration: a file that fixes the question, the metric and the pass line before the runs. If the result fails, we report it as failed. This has happened: safe RL did not meet its energy rule (chapter 8).

Settings are tuned on fold 0 (43 windows) only. The 160 test windows are not used for tuning.

Every car, including the recorded ones, goes through the same frozen BMW i3 battery model, so we compare driving behaviour, not different cars. Changes are paired: each window is compared with itself. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test checks whether the paired changes lean one way, without assuming a bell curve. We count p below 0.05.

  1. Write the rules first. The test, the metric and the pass line are fixed in a pre-registration file before the runs.
  2. Tune on fold 0. Settings are chosen on 43 windows only.
  3. Test once. The 160 held-out windows come from other days.
  4. Same battery model for every car, the recorded ones too. Changes are paired, window by window.

Chapter 7 of 10

Against real commercial-ACC cars, our car saves a little energy

We replay what each real follower did through the battery model, then compare it with our MPC car in the same traffic.

With a commercial ACC behind the lead car (79 windows), our car used 1.89% less energy (median). It was lower in 59 of 79 windows (p = 3.35e-09) with 0 unsafe windows. Against human drivers it saved 5.96% (40 windows).

Where we do not win. A simple tuned ACC saves more against the same recorded cars (2.82% against 1.89%), but it follows at 50.0 m instead of 40.0 m. Our MPC stays close to the recorded gap (41.3 m). Head to head over all 160 windows, our MPC uses 0.47% more energy than the tuned ACC. Only the oracle, which knows the future, is clearly ahead (5.49%). The size of our saving also depends on how we clean noise from the recorded speed. We fixed 1-second smoothing in advance (raw speed gives 5.88% less, 2-second smoothing 1.57% less).

Chapter 8 of 10

The safety filter fixed plain RL's safety, not its energy use

A window is called unsafe if the gap falls more than 0.5 m below the safe gap. Over the 160 test windows, our MPC, the tuned ACC, SMC and the oracle had 0 unsafe windows. Plain SAC had 2 with seed 42. Across its 3 training seeds (random starts) it had 2, 6 and 37.

With the safety filter added and the controller retrained, all three seeds had 0. The filter worked.

But it did not save energy. Safe SAC (seed 42) used 0.52% more energy than the tuned ACC, so it failed its energy rule. It follows farther back (43.2 m) and the filter stepped in on 24.15% of the test steps.

Comfort: jerk is how sharply the acceleration changes (m/s³; lower is smoother). Our MPC has jerk 0.27. The tuned ACC (0.21) and the oracle (0.22) are smoother. Plain SAC is the roughest (0.42).

Chapter 9 of 10

A separate MATLAB version gives the same answers

We re-wrote the battery model, the ACC and the MPC from scratch in MATLAB R2024b and ran 10 test windows. The website gives the same energy as MATLAB: the largest difference is 0.000% for the ACC and 0.002% for the MPC, with 0 solver failures. The paper’s Python differs by up to 0.159% because it used a different solver (OSQP).

This checks the code. It does not check the physics against a real car, and the same author wrote both versions. Only the ACC and the MPC with constant-speed preview were ported. In these 10 windows the MPC used more energy than the ACC in 8 and less in 2: the check is about agreement, not about who wins.

Chapter 10 of 10

What this shows, and what it does not

  • The car is simulated. Every energy number comes from one BMW i3 model. The lead car's recorded speed is replayed, and it does not react to our car. The regen cap of 35 kW is an assumption, not a measurement.
  • The gains are small. Our MPC saves a few percent against recorded cars, and it does not beat a tuned ACC on energy.
  • The oracle is a ceiling. It knows the future. Learned forecasts did not recover any of that saving.
  • One RL seed on the site. The paper runs three, and they differ a lot (plain SAC, median change against the tuned ACC from −0.03% to +1.46%).
  • Next. The paper is not yet published. The code, data and pre-registrations are public (see About).
